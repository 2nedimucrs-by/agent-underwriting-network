# AUN First-Pilot Buyer Discovery Guide

Use this guide for discovery interviews with AI platform, security, procurement, or agent-platform teams. The goal is to learn how they make a real decision today, not to pitch AUN or count polite interest as demand.

## Before the interview

- Choose a relevant role that has participated in an agent selection or approval decision.
- Ask permission to take notes. Do not record without explicit permission.
- Do not request confidential workflow details, credentials, customer data, or private repository access.
- Record only what the participant agrees can be retained; keep personal and company-sensitive notes outside this public repository.

## Questions

1. Tell me about the last time your team considered using or approving an AI agent for a real workflow.
2. What decision had to be made, who was involved, and what happened next?
3. What evidence did you use to make that decision? Which parts were missing or hard to verify?
4. What risks or costs mattered most, and what happened when the evidence was incomplete?
5. How often does this decision come up, and how much review effort does one case take?
6. What tools, vendors, internal checks, or manual processes do you use today?
7. Have you paid for help or tooling to make this kind of decision? What did you buy, and what budget or approval path did it use?
8. If you could change one part of that process, what would you change first?

Ask follow-up questions about specific past behavior, decisions, and spending. Avoid asking whether they “like” AUN, whether they would hypothetically buy it, or suggesting a price before understanding their process.

## Optional concept check

Only after understanding the current workflow, describe the narrow pilot concept: a human-reviewed, source-linked evidence pack for one named workflow and up to five exact, version-pinned agents, with unknowns clearly labeled and no safety certification or deployment authorization.

Then ask:

- What would be useful or missing for your actual decision?
- Who would need to review or approve this work?
- What would make the scope too broad or too risky?
- Is there a specific upcoming decision where a bounded pilot could help?
- What written scope, fee, and acceptance terms would procurement require?

If a participant expresses interest, record it as interest only. Treat willingness to pay as validated only after the buyer accepts a concrete scope and fee in writing through the appropriate business process.

## Evidence to retain privately

For each interview, record:

- date and participant role (use a private reference instead of a name in shared notes);
- the recent decision described and current process;
- evidence sources and gaps they identified;
- review frequency and effort, if they chose to share it;
- existing spend or budget process, if they chose to share it;
- exact follow-up agreed by the participant;
- whether they accepted a specific scope and price in writing.

Do not turn a favorable reaction into a customer commitment. A pilot is not commercially proven until the agreed work is delivered and accepted and the payment is confirmed.
